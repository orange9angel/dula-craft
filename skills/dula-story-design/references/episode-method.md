# Building a 7-10 minute comedy episode

A device or running gag is enough for a scene. To hold an episode, every answer the lead finds has to cause the
next problem. This method is a summary of practice described by writers of short animated comedy (sources at the end);
the timings are our own drafting targets.

## Steps

1. **Two people who would act today.** Give each a small, concrete want they could point to, put them in the same
   situation, and let one person's sensible move spoil the other's plan. Friends can want opposite things for one
   afternoon. A recognisable wish replaces exposition.
2. **Find the game.** The device is the fact the viewer learns; the game is what people keep doing because of it.
   Start with an ordinary attempt, show one odd result, then let somebody notice an opportunity in it. In the writers'
   room accept each result as true and ask what either person is now tempted to do ("yes, and"). Push the pattern
   further (heighten) and ask why someone would play along (explore).
3. **Three to five consequence cards.** Each card: "Because that worked / failed, ___ tries ___, which leaves ___."
   Raise a different pressure each time: a trick gives a private advantage; a witness spots it and exploits it; the
   lead hides it and gives the witness a better view; finally the lead must risk the very thing they spent the story
   preventing. If two middle cards can swap places without changing anything, one of them leaves too little behind.
4. **A plot first; earn any B plot.** The A plot owns the question the viewer stays for. A B plot earns its cutaways
   only if its result changes what the lead can do at the climax.
5. **Clock (for 8 minutes; scale for 7 or 10).**

| Time | The viewer gets |
|---|---|
| 0:00-0:15 | cold open: one ordinary action going wonderfully wrong; a question over the title |
| 0:15-0:50 | who wants what, who else has a claim, what opportunity is about to close; one short line names the oddity while someone uses it |
| 0:50-2:10 | the lead commits to a tactic; a real win; the other person sees how it works |
| 2:10-4:00 | the other adapts, the lead doubles down; by halfway the first success has made the plan harder |
| 4:00-5:55 | a changed tactic buys relief and spends the last advantage; worst moment: show what they stand to lose, hold a beat |
| 5:55-7:45 | the lead uses the familiar rule in a new way; the other's best move tests it; both wants resolve in a visible action |
| 7:45-8:00 | button: an early image returns with one changed detail; cut when the joke lands |

6. **What changed.** Read the cards as a story circle: where did success cost the lead something, and what do we see
   them choose at the end that they would have refused at the start - shown, not said.
7. **Test drawings and timing separately.** Read rough boards with dialogue covered and point to the instant the
   other person notices the trick. Then play timed boards with voices; cut lines that explain a laugh already had;
   look for dead stretches against a laugh or new comic image every 10-15 s. Ask a fresh viewer where they first got
   ahead, what the last attempt cost, and what made the ending possible.

## Failure tests

| Failure | Test question |
|---|---|
| Fixed gimmick | After the second demonstration, what does someone decide because they now understand the trick? |
| Errand plot | If the lead reached the destination at once, which contest between people would still need an ending? |
| Too many rules | Can a newcomer predict what the device does at the crisis from the first demonstration alone? |
| No second want | Left alone for 30 seconds, what would the other character try, and whose plan would it spoil? |
| Low stakes | If the lead walked away now, what small, specific loss would either person feel today? |
| Preachy virtue gag | Cut every mention of the good quality: what sight or sound still gets the laugh? |
| Set-driven story | With the list of available backgrounds hidden, would the character still choose this action? |
| Repetition posing as pace | Which two attempts could swap? What did the first need to teach the opponent? |
| Convenient rescue | Did the audience see the solution's ingredients before the lead chose to use them? |
